**Abstract** Site-specific herbicide application in sugarcane requires a detector that finds the weed present and supplies detections fast enough for the implement to act. Three deep learning detectors — Faster R-CNN, RetinaNet and YOLOv10 — were trained on 1,211 close-range field images of sugarcane and weeds from Tay Ninh province, Vietnam, and evaluated on a common test partition of 124 images under one protocol: one input resolution, 50 epochs, final-epoch weights and 4–5 runs per model. Scored under the confusion-matrix convention commonly reported in this literature, which discards undetected instances, all three separated the classes reliably (two-class mean F1-score 0.97-0.98). Scored so that undetected weed and spurious detections are retained, the same predictions gave weed-class precision of 0.44-0.59 and recall of 0.39-0.50; weighted by the ground area a sprayer would treat, recall was 0.50-0.69. Recall fell monotonically with weed size, from 0.19 in the smallest area quartile to 0.75 in the largest, and retraining the lightest detector at four times the pixel count did not change it. RetinaNet obtained the highest AP50 (57.0), but only YOLOv10 met the throughput required at 6 km h⁻¹, and it treated the least ground. Detection completeness, not class discrimination, limits this application, and the engineering lever is acquisition geometry rather than a better classifier.

**Keywords** Sugarcane; Weed detection; Site-specific herbicide application; Deep learning; Detection recall

## Introduction

Weeds are among the principal constraints on sugarcane yield and quality (Suganthi et al. 2019), competing with the crop for nutrients, light, water and space during establishment and acting as hosts for pests and diseases. The dominant control is herbicide, applied uniformly across the field, and the cost of that uniformity is both economic and environmental (Donga and Eklo 2018). Site-specific application — treating only the locations where weeds actually occur — reduces the quantity applied without reducing efficacy, and it is the motivation for putting a camera and a detection model on a sprayer.

That shifts the problem upstream, onto the detector, and it imposes two requirements that a laboratory accuracy figure does not express. The first is completeness: a weed patch the model does not find is a patch the implement drives past untreated, so what matters is the proportion of the weed present that is detected, not the proportion of detections that are correct. The second is throughput: detections must arrive fast enough that the implement does not travel past the target before the nozzle can act. At a working speed of 6 km h⁻¹, with successive frames required to advance no more than 0.30 m, the onboard hardware must supply at least 5.6 frames per second.

Deep learning object detectors are the natural candidates, and recent work in this crop has applied them to tasks closely related to the present one: stem base detection for intelligent harvesting (Li et al. 2026), detection of ratoon-emerged seedling hills for replanting (Mo et al. 2026), and smartphone diagnosis of foliar disease (Chen et al. 2026). For weed detection specifically, Sun et al. (2024) combined multi-scale convolution with a transformer backbone in sugarcane fields, Singh et al. (2024) performed pixel-level classification of weed patches from drone imagery, and Mini et al. (2020) applied Mask R-CNN to aerial sugarcane imagery. Reported accuracies in the wider weed-detection literature are consistently high, frequently above 98% (dos Santos Ferreira et al. 2017; Yu et al. 2019; Moazzam et al. 2021; Zhang et al. 2023), which would suggest the detection half of the problem is close to solved.

Whether that impression survives the two requirements above has not been tested for sugarcane on close-range imagery of the kind a boom-mounted camera would produce. The question is practical rather than architectural: not which detector is most accurate in the abstract, but how much of the weed in a sugarcane field a current detector actually finds, what limits that figure, and whether any of the candidates could run at the rate the implement demands.

This study addresses those questions on imagery collected under production conditions at a commercial farm in Tay Ninh province, Vietnam. It (i) builds and releases an annotated close-range dataset of sugarcane and its associated weed flora; (ii) trains and compares three detectors spanning the two-stage, single-stage dense and anchor-free real-time families — Faster R-CNN, RetinaNet and YOLOv10 — under a single protocol, at a single input resolution, with a single checkpoint rule and repeated training runs; (iii) reports detection completeness both by instance and by the ground area a sprayer would treat, together with the size dependence that governs it; and (iv) screens the three against the throughput the implement requires. Two further models trained on a polygon annotation of the same images are reported separately and are not ranked against the three.

## Materials and Methods

**Study area and image dataset.** The workflow from acquisition to evaluation is summarised in Fig. 1. Field images of sugarcane (cultivar KK3) and weeds were collected at Tan Hung farm, Chau Thanh district, Tay Ninh province, Vietnam, with a handheld iPhone 12 held about 140 cm above the ground, optical axis vertical. Acquisition took place in a single day, on 2025-03-20, in three sessions (09:09–09:59, 10:00–10:51, 13:17–13:44) on three separate plots, two with a recorded crop age of 40 and 60 days after planting and the third a mixed stand; the images are therefore correlated in illumination and in plot, and the dataset does not sample growth stage or season as a designed factor. The weed flora comprised purple nutsedge (*Cyperus rotundus* L.), Bermuda grass (*Cynodon dactylon* (L.) Pers.), crowfoot grass (*Dactyloctenium aegyptium* (L.) Willd.) and a fourth species known locally as ginger-leaf grass. In total 1,363 source images were annotated. The camera wrote 4080 × 3060 pixel frames, rescaled on export to a square 1280 × 1280 pixels without preserving the aspect ratio.

**Fig. 1** Workflow of the study, from image acquisition and annotation through model training to evaluation. The two annotation conventions run in parallel to separate test partitions and are never ranked against each other


**Image annotation and data augmentation.** All images were annotated on the Roboflow platform (Roboflow Inc., Des Moines, IA, USA) in two conventions, a bounding-box set and a polygon set, both classes in both. The bounding-box set draws on 1,211 source images and the polygon set on 504, 352 appearing in both. Each was divided 80:10:10 at the level of source images before augmentation, so no augmented copy of a training image can appear in a validation or test partition. Augmentation was applied to the training partition only, each source image yielding three versions generated by independently sampling a horizontal and a vertical flip each with probability 0.5, one of four 90° rotations with equal probability, a random crop of 0 to 20% of the image, brightness within ±15%, exposure within ±10%, and salt-and-pepper noise on 1.01% of pixels. Two consequences are not apparent from the image counts: the crop is a scale augmentation rather than a rigid transformation, so the three versions are not related by any exact axis-aligned mapping and in 226 of the 964 training source images (23%) they carry different instance counts because boxes cropped past the frame boundary are dropped on export; and all three are random draws, so the unmodified original is not itself retained. The bounding-box set therefore holds 2,892 training, 123 validation and 124 test images carrying 34,350 annotated instances, and the polygon set 1,212, 50 and 50 carrying 33,380. Augmentation internal to the training pipelines was disabled apart from horizontal flipping, so every model saw the same data.

**Scope of the comparison.** The two annotation sets are not interchangeable, and this governs what can be compared. Their test partitions share only three images, and they annotate the same scenes at different granularity: the polygon set marks 29.3 instances per test image against 10.6 for the bounding-box set, with a median weed instance occupying 0.31% of the frame against 1.38%. One convention delineates weed patches, the other resolves them into constituent parts, so a metric computed under one is not comparable with the same metric under the other. The comparison reported here is accordingly restricted to the three models trained on the bounding-box convention, which share a single test partition of 124 images and an identical protocol. The two polygon-trained models are reported separately and are not ranked against them.

**Object detection models.** Five models were trained, spanning the three detector families named in the Introduction. Faster R-CNN (Ren et al. 2017) generates candidate regions with a Region Proposal Network, then classifies and refines them; Mask R-CNN (He et al. 2017) extends it to instance segmentation. RetinaNet (Lin et al. 2017) predicts densely in one stage and addresses foreground–background imbalance with Focal Loss. YOLOv8-seg (Jocher et al. 2023) is anchor-free and single-stage, and YOLOv10 (Wang et al. 2024) additionally removes the need for non-maximum suppression at inference. The three Detectron2 models used a ResNet-50 backbone (He et al. 2016) with a Feature Pyramid Network (Lin et al. 2017b); all five were initialised from COCO-pretrained weights (Lin et al. 2014).

**Model training configuration.** All models were trained on one workstation with an NVIDIA GeForce RTX 3080 Ti (12 GB VRAM). Faster R-CNN, Mask R-CNN and RetinaNet used Detectron2 v0.6 (Wu et al. 2019) with PyTorch 2.1.2 (Paszke et al. 2019); YOLOv8-seg and YOLOv10 used Ultralytics v8.3 (Jocher et al. 2023). Training ran for 50 epochs with AdamW (Loshchilov and Hutter 2019), an initial learning rate of 0.001, cosine annealing (Loshchilov and Hutter 2017), weight decay 0.0001, a three-epoch warm-up and an effective batch of 16 (Table 1) realised by gradient accumulation. The learning rate was not tuned per architecture, so that the architectures rather than the search effort spent on each are what differ. No early stopping and no checkpoint selection were applied: every model was trained for the full schedule and the final-epoch weights evaluated, because the two frameworks default differently and taking each default would have given one model a selection advantage. Inference settings are in Supplementary Table S1 of Online Resource 1; one binds, in that RetinaNet reached its 100-detection cap on 14 to 18 of the 124 test images depending on the seed, against medians of 14 and 9 detections per image for the other two, so its average precision is bounded by that cap. Training curves are shown in Supplementary Figure S1.

**Evaluation metrics.** Two families of metric are reported, because they answer different questions and the conclusions depend on both. The first is average precision under the COCO protocol (Lin et al. 2014): AP averaged over intersection-over-union thresholds from 0.50 to 0.95, with AP50 and AP75. These penalise missed objects and spurious detections together. The second is derived from a confusion matrix over matched detections, the family most often quoted in applied weed-detection studies, and is reported here in two variants that differ only in whether the background row and column are kept. Predictions scoring at most 0.30 are discarded; each remaining prediction is matched to at most one ground-truth instance with an IoU above 0.50, matches assigned in order of decreasing IoU; unmatched ground truth and unmatched predictions go to a background row and column. In the *background-excluded* variant those are deleted before the metrics are computed, so the only errors counted are instances given the wrong class: the variant answers whether a localised object is labelled correctly and cannot answer how many objects were found. In the *full* variant both are retained, so an undetected instance is a false negative and a detection with no matching ground truth a false positive. Both variants are reported for Precision, Recall and F1-score and for both classes. Since both are defined at one confidence and one IoU threshold, and confidence scores are not calibrated to a common scale across frameworks, the sensitivity to both is reported in the Results. Precision, Recall, F1-score and Accuracy are computed in the usual way from the counts of true positives, false positives and false negatives of each class.

**Statistical design.** Two sources of variability are quantified. The finite test set is addressed by bootstrap: metrics are recomputed on 1000 resamples of the 124 test images drawn with replacement, with differences between models taken within an iteration so that the correlation between models scored on identical images is preserved. Training variability is addressed by repetition: each bounding-box model was trained repeatedly from different random seeds with data, schedule and hyperparameters held fixed, yielding 5, 4, 5 converged runs respectively, and seed-level values are compared with Welch's t-test, the three pairwise P-values adjusted by the Holm–Bonferroni procedure and effect sizes given as Hedges' g with a 95% interval. Each of these holds one source fixed, so they are combined in a two-level bootstrap that resamples test images and training runs together and takes each difference within an iteration; that interval is quoted wherever this paper states that two architectures do or do not differ. A variance-sum approximation was considered and rejected, because it assumes the two sources are independent and additive on the metric scale, which does not hold for a ratio such as Recall near the middle of its range. The two polygon-trained models were trained once each, so no comparison involving them is tested.

**Inference-speed measurement and deployment screening.** A detector for an autonomous sprayer must supply detections at a rate set by the forward speed of the implement. At 6 km/h with successive frames advancing no more than 0.30 m, the deployment hardware must deliver at least 5.6 frames per second — a requirement derived from the agronomy of the operation, not from the measured throughputs. An embedded accelerator of the class intended here delivers roughly one tenth to one fifth of a desktop GPU's throughput (NVIDIA 2023), a ratio taken from vendor specifications and not measured here, so a model must reach about 56 frames per second on the workstation to remain plausible under the more conservative assumption. Throughput was measured over ten repeated passes of a 40-image subset of the test set, discarding the first ten inferences, timing the complete per-image path at batch size 1 in single precision with the GPU synchronised after every image. Compute is quoted in GMACs because the two frameworks report it under different conventions and mixing them understates the difference between architectures twofold. The screen is a necessary and not a sufficient condition — confirming feasibility needs the target hardware, which was unavailable — and it addresses throughput alone, where a working implement is also constrained by end-to-end latency and nozzle actuation delay, neither measured here.

**How much weed is found.** The confusion matrix over matched detections can be scored in two ways, and Table 2 gives both for the same predictions. Deleting the background row and column before computing the metrics — the convention most often quoted in this literature — counts only instances given the wrong one of the two classes, and on that basis all three detectors separated sugarcane from weeds reliably, with two-class mean F1-scores from 0.967 to 0.982. Retaining the background row and column, so that an undetected instance counts as a false negative and a detection with no matching annotation as a false positive, gives for the weed class Precision of 0.436, 0.498, and 0.591, Recall of 0.501, 0.480, and 0.388 and F1-score of 0.466, 0.489, and 0.468 for RetinaNet, Faster R-CNN, and YOLOv10 respectively. **Where the difference comes from.** Fig. 2 shows the confusion matrices behind both columns of Table 2, with the band that the background-excluded convention removes marked. Taking RetinaNet as the worked example: of the 894 annotated weed instances in the test partition, 440 were detected and classified as weed, 8 were detected but called sugarcane, and 446 produced no detection at all. In the other direction the model placed 550 weed boxes where no instance was annotated and 18 on sugarcane. Scoring with the whole matrix gives a weed-class F1-score of 0.46. Deleting the background row and column first removes 446 false negatives and 550 false positives from the arithmetic — the two largest off-diagonal entries — and the same predictions then score 0.97.

The deleted band is not a technicality. Its column holds the weed the implement would drive past untreated and its row holds the ground it would spray for nothing; between them they are the whole of the field performance. What survives deletion is the question of whether an object already found is labelled correctly, which these models answer almost perfectly and which no sprayer asks. The three matrices also show the behavioural difference reported earlier: the background row grows from 801 for RetinaNet, 597 for Faster R-CNN, and 313 for YOLOv10 spurious detections, in the same order as the number of boxes each model proposes.

**What a sprayer would actually treat.** Counting instances is not the same as covering ground, and for an implement the second is the operative quantity. Weighting each annotated weed instance by its bounding-box area rather than counting it once gives a recall of 0.692 for RetinaNet, 0.642 for Faster R-CNN, and 0.501 for YOLOv10, against 0.513, 0.488, and 0.392 by instance count — 11 to 18 percentage points higher in every case, because the instances that go undetected are the small ones. Both figures are reported: the instance count measures how many weed patches the system is blind to, the area-weighted figure how much of the weed problem a sprayer driven by these detections would treat.

**The whole operating curve, not one point.** Every figure above is taken at a single confidence threshold of 0.30. Fig. 3 shows the complete trade-off. Fig. 3a gives the precision-recall curve of the weed class in the full variant: the three architectures trace closely spaced curves, differing in precision by a median of 0.075 over the recall range they share, and they differ mainly in where on that common trade-off each one sits rather than in how favourable the trade-off is. Fig. 3b restates it in the units a sprayer operator would use, recall against false weed detections per image. At the working threshold the models produce 4.6 for RetinaNet, 3.5 for Faster R-CNN, and 1.9 for YOLOv10 spurious weed detections per image, and buying further recall is expensive: RetinaNet reaches 0.59 only at 29.5 false detections per image, Faster R-CNN reaches 0.52 only at 5.2 false detections per image, and YOLOv10 reaches 0.47 only at 3.5 false detections per image.

Fig. 3c asks whether the confidence score itself can separate correct weed detections from spurious ones. This is the one place in a detection problem where a receiver operating characteristic is well defined, because the negatives are a finite, identifiable set — the weed detections that match no annotated weed — rather than the unbounded set of image locations containing no object. The areas under the curve are 0.84 for RetinaNet, 0.76 for Faster R-CNN, and 0.73 for YOLOv10. Separation is moderate, not strong, and that is the reason the spurious detections cannot simply be thresholded away: raising the confidence threshold discards correct detections at almost the same rate as incorrect ones, which is why the F1-score in Supplementary Table S2 is nearly flat across the threshold range for two of the three models.

**What limits detection.** Weed-class Recall falls monotonically with object size across the whole range, not only among the smallest instances. Dividing the 894 annotated weed instances of the test partition into area quartiles, with boundaries at 9,092 px², 22,606 px², and 53,437 px² on the 1280 px exported frame, Recall rises from 0.233 to 0.696 for Faster R-CNN, 0.237 to 0.754 for RetinaNet, and 0.188 to 0.533 for YOLOv10. The gradient is steeper still under the absolute COCO size bands (Supplementary Table S4), though those place only 42 of the 894 instances in the smallest category. The missed instances are not, in absolute terms, tiny: the smallest quartile reaches 95 px on a side and its median instance is larger than the threshold at which general-purpose benchmarks stop calling an object small. What makes them hard is their size combined with the resizing applied at the network input and the visual similarity of weed and crop foliage.

Part of that shortfall is imprecise localisation rather than failure to see the object: relaxing the matching threshold from an IoU of 0.50 to 0.10 at the same confidence (Supplementary Table S3) raises Recall from 0.480 to 0.614 for Faster R-CNN, 0.501 to 0.632 for RetinaNet, and 0.388 to 0.481 for YOLOv10, so about a quarter of the apparent shortfall is a box that overlapped its annotation too little to count. A second correction works the same way: for a sprayer the quantity that matters is the weed-covered ground treated, not the instances found, and the two differ because the undetected instances are the small ones. Weighting each instance by its area gives 0.642 for Faster R-CNN, 0.692 for RetinaNet, and 0.501 for YOLOv10 against 0.488, 0.513, and 0.392 by instance count.

Recall also varies with plot and time of acquisition. Scored separately by capture session (Supplementary Table S9), the mean weed-class Recall across the three models is 0.554 on the 10h (60-day plot) and 0.373 on the 09h (40-day plot) — a spread wider than any difference between the architectures, with the model ordering not stable across sessions. Because the sessions differ simultaneously in plot, crop age and illumination, this experiment cannot attribute the spread to any one of them; what it establishes is that a single pooled figure conceals variation of that magnitude.

All the above use the confidence threshold of 0.30 inherited from earlier work, and confidence scores are not calibrated to a common scale across frameworks. Sweeping it from 0.05 to 0.90 (Supplementary Table S2) leaves the weed-class F1-score of the two Detectron2 models nearly flat while that of YOLOv10 falls from 0.474 to 0.357. Faster R-CNN has the highest weed-class F1-score at every threshold in that range, so the ordering is not an artefact of the value chosen, though the size of the differences is.

**Is the difference an effect of input resolution?** YOLOv10 is the lightest model and, at its default 640 × 640 input, the one that recovers fewest weed instances — exactly what a resolution limitation would predict. To separate the two explanations it was retrained from scratch at 1024 × 1024, the input size of the other two models, for 3 seeds under an otherwise identical protocol, the effective batch preserved by gradient accumulation. It did not help: AP50 was 49.23 ± 0.77 against 49.83 ± 0.60 at 640 × 640, weed-class Recall 0.394 against 0.388, and weed-class F1-score 0.468 against 0.468. Quadrupling the pixel count changed none of these by more than the spread between seeds. The recall shortfall of this architecture is therefore not an artefact of its input size; capacity, or the way the anchor-free head assigns targets, is the remaining explanation, and this experiment does not separate those. It also bears on the engineering recommendation: a larger network input alone is not the remedy, and camera height or tiling, which change the pixels falling on a weed patch rather than the pixels fed to the network, are the more promising route.

**Inference cost and deployment screening.** Throughput on the workstation GPU was 23.0 frames per second for Faster R-CNN, 23.4 frames per second for RetinaNet, and 216.3 frames per second for YOLOv10. Against the screening threshold of 56 frames per second derived in the Methods, only YOLOv10 clears it; Faster R-CNN and RetinaNet fall below the required rate even assuming the embedded device delivers one fifth of workstation throughput. Table 3 gives the corresponding architectural cost: Faster R-CNN 41.3 million parameters and 206 GMACs, RetinaNet 36.3 million parameters and 204 GMACs, and YOLOv10 8.1 million parameters and 12 GMACs, computed at each model's own input resolution and therefore overstating the ratio at a common one. The screen is a necessary and not a sufficient condition.

The throughput advantage is not bought by the lower input resolution alone. Measured under the same protocol at 1024 × 1024, the resolution of the other two models, YOLOv10 still reached 102 frames per second (9.8 ms per image), well above the 56 frames per second required, while its detection quality was unchanged. The separation between this architecture and the two ResNet-50 models survives equalising the input size on both counts.

**Statistics.** Two sources of variability bear on every comparison. Resampling the 124 test images with replacement (1000 resamples) gives 95% intervals on AP50 of Faster R-CNN [49.9, 56.8], RetinaNet [53.8, 60.1], and YOLOv10 [46.8, 54.0] and on weed-class Recall in the full variant of [0.424, 0.525], [0.449, 0.539], and [0.357, 0.447] — wide relative to the differences between models. Across seeds, AP50 was Faster R-CNN 53.60 ± 1.42, RetinaNet 57.05 ± 0.15, and YOLOv10 49.83 ± 0.60. For every model the test-set interval is the larger of the two, so the dominant uncertainty comes from how few images were held out.

Holding the test partition fixed and comparing seed-level values with Welch's t-test (n = 5 for Faster R-CNN, 4 for RetinaNet, 5 for YOLOv10), the three P-values of each metric adjusted by the Holm–Bonferroni procedure, the differences remaining significant at the 5% level are all of three for AP50, all of three for weed-class Recall, and two of three for weed-class F1-score (Supplementary Table S7, which also gives Hedges' g with its interval for all nine comparisons).

Each of those holds one source fixed, and a claim that one architecture detects better than another needs both released. A two-level bootstrap — resampling test images and training runs together, each difference taken within an iteration — gives intervals excluding zero in 2 of the 6 comparisons: Recall, Faster R-CNN against YOLOv10: +0.094 [+0.046, +0.144] and Recall, RetinaNet against YOLOv10: +0.114 [+0.069, +0.164] (Supplementary Table S8). The two architectures with the larger backbones therefore detect reliably more weed instances than the third, while no difference in F1-score between any pair survives both sources. Where this paper states that architectures are not separable, that is the interval it refers to.

One methodological choice is large enough to change a conclusion. All models were evaluated at final-epoch weights; evaluating YOLOv10 instead at the epoch scoring best on validation — its framework's default, which the other two do not offer — raises AP50 from 49.83 ± 0.60 to 52.32 ± 3.73 (Supplementary Table S6), 2.5 points of mean and a 6-fold larger standard deviation. An earlier version of this analysis used each framework's default and called YOLOv10 the least reproducible of the three; under the symmetric protocol it is the most reproducible.

Training stability differed sharply: 3 of the 7 runs attempted for RetinaNet diverged with a non-finite classification loss within the first 300 iterations at the learning rate of 0.001 used throughout, while the others converged on every attempt. Divergent runs were excluded and further seeds drawn, but drawing was stopped rather than continued to a target count, because re-drawing only the architecture that fails would make the surviving sample a product of that selection; the standard deviation above for that architecture is therefore conditioned on convergence. For symmetry, in 2 of the 5 YOLOv10 runs the log shows a non-finite box-regression loss at one or two late epochs, from which the run recovered and completed normally.

Two cautions apply to the analysis itself: 4–5 runs per model is a small sample, so the tests have low power and a comparison that does not reach significance should be read as inconclusive rather than as evidence of equivalence; and the whole analysis is conditional on one test partition from a single day's acquisition.

**Models trained on the polygon convention.** For completeness, Mask R-CNN and YOLOv8-seg were evaluated on the polygon test partition of 50 images under the same protocol, reaching AP50 of 39.4 and 37.7 respectively (Supplementary Table S10). Those values are computed against ground truth that resolves weed patches into constituent parts and describe a different task; each model was trained once, so no training variability is available for them.

**Qualitative results.** Fig. 4 shows, for each model, one test image on which detection succeeded and one on which it failed, with the ground-truth annotation for the same frames; the pairs were chosen by per-image weed-class Recall on the first run, at the median and the tenth percentile. ## Discussion

**Why published figures look better than field performance.** The two sets of columns in Table 2 come from one set of predictions on one set of images and differ by roughly a factor of two. The difference is a reporting convention. When the background row and column of the confusion matrix are deleted, an annotated weed that produced no detection leaves the denominator with them, and so does a detection that matched no annotation. What remains measures one thing: given that an object has been localised, is it labelled correctly? That is a reasonable question and the answer here is encouraging, a two-class mean F1-score between 0.97 and 0.98. It is not the question a sprayer asks, and a figure of that magnitude quoted without stating which convention produced it will be read as though it were. On the same predictions the full variant gives weed-class F1-scores between 0.47 and 0.49. The convention is widespread and its effect is large enough to change what a reader concludes about whether the problem is solved.

**Comparison with detection results reported in sugarcane.** Published figures for this crop are mostly not commensurable with the present ones. Singh et al. (2024) report 90.5% accuracy for pixel-level classification of weed patches from drone imagery, a per-pixel figure for a segmentation task at a different acquisition scale; Mini et al. (2020) report an AP50 of 65.5% with Mask R-CNN against mask annotations on aerial imagery. By the same rule this study applies to its own two annotation conventions, neither can be placed on one scale with the AP50 of 57.0% for RetinaNet, 53.6% for Faster R-CNN, and 49.8% for YOLOv10 obtained here. The direction is nonetheless worth stating: close-range nadir acquisition at 140 cm did not by itself yield higher average precision than the published aerial result, which is evidence against the assumption that moving the camera closer resolves the detection problem.

**Implications for sprayer design.** Three consequences follow for anyone building the implement. First, the binding constraint is detection, not classification: once a weed patch is localised its class is almost always assigned correctly, and the counts of sugarcane reported as weed (18, 12, and 11) and weed reported as sugarcane (8, 8, and 7) are negligible beside the numbers undetected; of those two, sugarcane reported as weed is the more harmful, because that plant is sprayed. Second, the shortfall has two halves that cost differently — weed left untreated, and herbicide applied where no weed was annotated — and a single accuracy figure hides the trade-off between them. Third, the engineering lever is acquisition geometry rather than architecture: recall falls monotonically with object size, and retraining the lightest model at the input resolution of the other two did not change its detection quality, so lowering the camera or tiling the frame, which change the pixels falling on a weed patch, are the indicated routes.

**The ranking is a property of the yardstick, not only of the model.** Across the 11 measures collected in Table 4, each of the three architectures ranks first on at least one, and the two that lead most often do so on 5 measures for RetinaNet, 5 measures for YOLOv10, and 1 measures for Faster R-CNN. A reader who takes AP50 as the criterion concludes that RetinaNet is the better detector; one who takes AP75 or weed-class precision concludes the opposite; one who takes weed-class F1-score in the full variant concludes that neither leads. All three readings are correct about their own measure, and none of them is correct about the field.

The pattern behind the split is mechanical rather than mysterious, and it is visible in how many detections each model returns: medians of 14, 50 and 9 per image for Faster R-CNN, RetinaNet and YOLOv10. A detector that proposes freely finds more of what is present and is wrong more often, so it leads on recall, on average recall and on the area-weighted measure, and trails on precision. A detector that proposes sparingly is right more often and misses more, so it leads on precision, on the background-excluded F1-score and on the stricter overlap threshold. Neither behaviour is better in the abstract; which is better depends on whether a weed left untreated or herbicide applied to bare ground is the more expensive error on a given farm. That is an agronomic and economic question, not a question about architectures, and it cannot be settled by reporting one number. The practical recommendation that follows is to state the operating point and the convention alongside any figure quoted for this task, and to choose the model against the cost structure of the intended operation rather than against a leaderboard.

**Choosing between the three.** No architecture was best on every criterion, and the honest outcome is a stated trade-off. YOLOv10 was the only model to clear the throughput screen, and it also recovered the fewest weed instances and the smallest treated area; the two architectures that found more are, on this hardware, too slow at the assumed working speed. Where completeness governs, the heavier architectures are indicated and the throughput problem must be solved another way — a slower working speed, a wider field of view, or a dedicated accelerator. Where onboard throughput binds, the lighter architecture is the only candidate and the weed it misses must be accepted or handled by a second pass. The area-weighted figures are the ones to use in that calculation: 0.69 for RetinaNet, 0.64 for Faster R-CNN, and 0.50 for YOLOv10 of the weed-covered ground would be treated.

**Limitations.** The acquisition bounds everything reported here. All 1,210 source images carrying a timestamp were recorded on a single day, 2025-03-20, within 4.6 hours, in three sessions on three plots of one farm with one cultivar, so a test partition drawn at random from it measures generalisation to unseen frames of the same plots under the same light and nothing beyond. Whether frames two seconds apart are near-duplicates, which would leak training content into the test partition, was checked rather than assumed: matching ORB features under a RANSAC homography, pairs within five seconds share a median inlier fraction of 0.006 against 0.005 for pairs more than ten minutes apart, and only 4 of 60 close-in-time pairs exceed five per cent. There is therefore no evidence of duplicate leakage; the correlation that exists is one of condition.

The comparison covers three architectures rather than five, because the two annotation conventions were never reconciled; deriving boxes from the polygon annotations and retraining all five under one convention is the single change that would most strengthen the design. One asymmetry remains among the three: YOLOv10 carries a fifth of the parameters of the two ResNet-50 models, so a difference between them confounds architecture with capacity. A single learning rate was used throughout, chosen before any result was seen, which keeps the comparison from rewarding whichever model received more tuning but also means no architecture is shown at its best.

## Conclusion

Three deep learning detectors were trained on close-range field imagery of sugarcane and weeds from Tay Ninh province, Vietnam, and evaluated against the two requirements a site-specific sprayer imposes. All three assigned the correct class to almost every object they localised, with two-class mean F1-scores from 0.97 to 0.98 under the convention commonly reported in this literature. Scored so that undetected weed and spurious detections are retained, the same predictions gave weed-class Precision of 0.44 to 0.59 and Recall of 0.39 to 0.50; weighted by the ground area a sprayer would treat, recall was 0.50 to 0.69.

Detection completeness, not class discrimination, is therefore what limits this application, and it is governed by object size: recall rose from 0.19-0.24 in the smallest quartile of annotated weed instances to 0.53-0.75 in the largest. Retraining the lightest detector at four times the pixel count left its detection quality unchanged, so the remedy indicated is acquisition geometry — camera height or tiled inference — rather than a larger network input or a better classifier. Of the three, only YOLOv10 met the throughput the implement requires at 6 km h⁻¹, and it is also the model that treats the least ground, so the choice is a trade-off rather than a ranking. Confirming feasibility on a working implement requires measurement on the target hardware, which this study did not have.
